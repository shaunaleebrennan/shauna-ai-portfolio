01 Find the insight

Start where the market is.
Decide where it should go.

Use this playbook when people understand what you sell but miss why the issue deserves attention, when a category’s familiar story hides your value, or when the market has moved and your story needs to catch up.

Define the job first.One audience. One situation. One decision you want to influence.

Before writing, establish who you need to reach, what they are trying to achieve, what they do today, and what your product can actually deliver. Bring the alternatives buyers consider, the value of your differences, and the evidence behind them.

Positioning establishes who should choose the product, over which alternatives, and why. Narrative develops a view of what matters and how the audience should respond. Messaging gives teams useful ways to express that argument.

Diagnose the issue fairly. An unclear category, an undifferentiated product, or a missing capability may need a strategic decision before it needs a new story.

Customer signal

Inspect recent decisions, workarounds, objections, and moments of friction. Listen for the assumptions behind what people do, including reasons their current approach makes sense.

Competitive signal

Compare the stories and criteria the market repeats. Include internal builds, existing tools, and doing nothing. A different headline can still make the same argument.

Analyst & market signal

Use outside perspectives to investigate emerging expectations and market direction. Test how those patterns appear in your audience’s work before turning them into a claim.

Separate what you see
from what you infer.

Write the observation, your interpretation, and the implication separately. For each candidate insight, ask what supports it, what contradicts it, and which decision it would change. Compare a few plausible explanations before choosing the one that makes the neatest story.

How to choose an insight worth building around

Look for a pattern that is consequential for a defined audience and supported in a recognizable situation. Specify what changed, compared with when, and for whom. A broad trend can provide context; it still needs a connection to the buyer’s decision.

One interview can expose a useful hypothesis. Investigate whether it recurs, where it does not, and whether another explanation fits better. The goal is a defensible judgment, with enough support for the use you intend.

Ask a capable critic: “If this interpretation is wrong, what would we expect to find?” Assign the unresolved research rather than settling it through the most persuasive voice in the room.

What if there is no meaningful market change?

A persistent problem can be misunderstood without being new. Use a reframe-led story when a contradiction exposes the limits of the accepted understanding. Develop the situation, contradiction, new interpretation, practical response, product contribution, and next action.

This is a practical alternative used in this playbook. Marcus Andrews’ five-stage method below is a change-led arc. Choose the structure the evidence supports. A product release alone does not establish that the customer’s world has changed.

02 Build the argument

Five beats.
One connected argument.

Marcus Andrews’ change-led arc starts in the audience’s world. The product enters once the audience understands the approach and the obstacles to adopting it.

Follow one fictional onboarding example through all five stages. In this scenario, a SaaS company has moved from single-team customers to enterprise rollouts. The scenario and product capabilities are illustrative.

The world movesWhat changes for us?
Find a shift the audience can recognize.

The Change

What has changed—and what makes that matter now?

Look outside the company for a consequential shift in behavior, expectations, economics, technology use, or how work gets done. Show the observation before asking the audience to accept the interpretation.

  • Define the audience, timeframe, and situation. “AI is changing everything” is too broad to do this work.
  • Distinguish a new technical possibility from actual adoption and its consequences.
  • Use customer behavior, product use, credible research, or concrete examples. Investigate contrary cases.
Worked example · The Change

“Your customers used to get started within one team. Now an enterprise rollout needs security approval, a data integration, and several teams to be ready together.”

Why this works & what to test

The reasoning. Starts with a specific change in the customer’s work. It gives the story a reason to exist beyond a product launch.

The pressure test. Has onboarding actually become more interdependent for the buyers you want to reach? Compare recent journeys with earlier ones.

The output: A specific change, the audience it affects, and supporting evidence.

The Impact

What does the change mean for this audience’s success?

Stay with the consequences long enough to make them matter. Explain what becomes possible, harder, more expensive, expected, or risky. Connect an operational effect to an outcome someone owns.

  • Show the mechanism between the change and the consequence. Urgency words cannot supply that connection.
  • Include opportunity alongside the cost of continuing as they are.
  • Give the stakes a human scale: a missed handoff, an unresolved question, or an ambition someone cannot achieve.
Worked example · The Impact

“Each team can finish its checklist while the customer still cannot use what they bought. Until those pieces come together, the value they signed up for stays out of reach.”

Why this works & what to test

The reasoning. Connects added dependencies to a consequence the customer recognizes. It changes what “complete” needs to mean.

The pressure test. Where does a finished internal task still leave the customer blocked? Look for a real handoff and its consequence.

The output: A consequence that gives this audience a reason to reconsider.

The New Game

What should people do differently to succeed?

This is the strategic center of the narrative. Turn your point of view into an approach a practitioner can use. Develop the substance before choosing a name.

  • Define the principle, the actions it changes, and what success looks like.
  • Compare it fairly with the current approach. Acknowledge the work or trade-off it introduces.
  • Make the method useful before a product purchase. “Use our software” leaves the strategic work unfinished.
Worked example · The New Game

“Plan onboarding around the customer’s first valuable outcome. Agree what that outcome is, work back to the milestones that enable it, and measure progress against them.”

Why this works & what to test

The reasoning. Offers a practical approach before introducing software. A team can try it with the tools it already has.

The pressure test. Can a team name the outcome, identify its dependencies, and tell whether the customer has reached it?

The output: An approach people can put into practice and a clear measure of success.

Why It’s Hard

If the approach makes sense, what gets in the way?

Agreement with an idea does not remove the difficulty of acting on it. Investigate ownership, incentives, skills, budget, data, permissions, integration, and workflow dependencies where relevant.

  • Separate familiar frustrations from barriers to adopting the proposed approach.
  • Include costs and risks created by the change itself.
  • Recognize capable people working within real constraints. Some obstacles require process, services, or organizational change.
Worked example · Why It’s Hard

“That takes a shared view of work spread across customer success, IT, and the customer’s own team. When a requirement is missing, someone needs to see the gap and know who can resolve it.”

Why this works & what to test

The reasoning. Explains what makes the new approach difficult. The obstacle follows from the method rather than appearing to justify a feature.

The pressure test. Do customers struggle to see the gap, resolve ownership, or both? Software alone will not settle unclear responsibilities.

The output: The barriers to adoption and the limits of what the product can address.

The Solution

How do you help people put the approach into practice?

Give the product a clear job. Trace the relevant capabilities to the obstacles already established, explain the mechanism, and show proof appropriate to the outcome.

  • Establish why the product is a credible choice against the buyer’s actual alternatives.
  • Distinguish delivered capabilities, configuration, pilots, and plans.
  • Show what the customer can do, what the result depends on, and the next useful action.
Worked example · The Solution

“Our onboarding software brings requirements, owners, and milestone progress into one shared view. AI flags missing information so the responsible person can investigate what is holding the customer back.”

Why this works & what to test

The reasoning. Gives the product a defined job: make gaps visible and actionable. The customer’s first valuable outcome remains the goal.

The pressure test. Can the product identify the relevant gap and help the right person act? Demonstrate that workflow before claiming faster onboarding.

The output: A clear connection between the obstacle, product capability, outcome, and proof.

Developing the work
and telling the story.

Your development process moves through research, choices, drafting, activation, and testing. The five beats above describe the audience’s experience of a change-led story. Keep that distinction clear so the process does not become another competing narrative template.

Build a new approach with substance

Write down what a practitioner would start doing, stop doing, and measure differently. Make the actions concrete enough to try. Explain what the approach prioritizes and what it gives up.

A memorable name can help people repeat an idea. A new software category is a separate strategic choice, with implications for buyer understanding, education, resources, and leadership commitment. Neither is required for every narrative.

Authority develops through useful education, implementation guidance, customer outcomes, and sustained execution. A coined phrase alone does not establish ownership of an idea.

Give the story momentum

Establish a recognizable situation, let the tension develop, and earn the turning point. Use contrast between present conditions and what could become possible. Return to that contrast when it helps an audience see the next implication.

Read the argument aloud. Mark where a listener would need an explanation, where the stakes feel inflated, and where a capability arrives before its purpose is clear. Keep each transition understandable on first hearing.

The customer has the ambition and makes the choices. The company contributes a credible way to help. A useful scene or demonstration can make that relationship more tangible than another abstract claim about transformation.

03 See it in practice

A completed checklist.
A customer still waiting.

Read the same five-stage example as one connected story. The fictional scenario shows how a change in the customer’s work leads to a different approach and a specific role for the product.

The five stages, together
The starting message

“An AI-powered platform for smarter customer onboarding.”

This names the technology and the activity. The narrative needs an argument about what successful onboarding requires.

The strategic choice

Move the evaluation from internal task completion toward the customer’s first valuable outcome. Then show how requirements, ownership, and progress help teams work toward it.

What would we need to establish?

Establish whether target customers have moved toward more complex rollouts, which dependencies delay value, and how they handle those dependencies today.

Then demonstrate the proposed product capabilities and test whether they help customers progress. This fictional example supplies no customer research or measured results.

The complete narrative · Illustrative

Your customers used to get started within one team. Now an enterprise rollout needs security approval, a data integration, and several teams to be ready together.

Each team can finish its checklist while the customer still cannot use what they bought. Until those pieces come together, the value they signed up for stays out of reach.

Plan onboarding around the customer’s first valuable outcome. Agree what that outcome is, work back to the milestones that enable it, and measure progress against them.

That takes a shared view of work spread across customer success, IT, and the customer’s own team. When a requirement is missing, someone needs to see the gap and know who can resolve it.

Our onboarding software brings requirements, owners, and milestone progress into one shared view. AI flags missing information so the responsible person can investigate what is holding the customer back.

Start with one customer journey. Can you trace every onboarding activity to the outcome the customer is waiting for?

Explore the decisions behind the story.

Test the reason to choose.

A buyer may accept the new approach and still use an existing tool. That response can validate the usefulness of the idea while leaving the product’s differentiated contribution unresolved. Show why your offering helps this audience execute the approach more effectively.

04 Apply & test

Make the promise traceable.

Keep the evidence for the market change, the recommended approach, the product contribution, and business impact distinct. A compelling story needs the right support at each connection.

Obstacle

A person cannot see what is blocking onboarding.

Capability

The product flags missing information.

Mechanism

The responsible person can investigate and address the gap.

Outcome

The customer can progress toward the agreed milestone.

Evidence

Verify the behavior and the resulting progress in the relevant workflow.

The proof must fit
the claim.

A demonstration can show a behavior under particular conditions. A customer reaction can show perceived relevance. A measured workflow can show whether a task completed. Each answers a different question.

When making an outcome claim, explain what succeeded, for whom, under which conditions, and how it was assessed. Include the important dependencies. For AI, account for review effort, exceptions, and the difference between producing an answer and completing useful work.

Keep the ambition. Check the connection. A future view can be bold while remaining clear about the observations, interpretations, and assumptions behind it.

Pressure-test the argument.

Start with the earliest weak assumption. Repair what follows from it before polishing the language.

Does the audience recognize it?

Ask buyers to reconstruct a recent situation or decision. Look for the tension in their experience, including cases where your explanation does not fit.

If they understand but see little relevance, revisit the audience, situation, and stakes.

Does the approach change a choice?

Ask what they would prioritize, measure, or do differently. Let them retell the idea without coaching or borrowing the headline.

If they repeat a benefit but miss the point of view, revisit the turning point and method.

Can the product earn the promise?

Test a realistic workflow against the actual alternative. Check the mechanism, dependencies, and outcome you intend to claim.

If the connection fails, narrow the promise, strengthen the proof, or identify a product decision.

What would a capable critic say?

Build the strongest credible response from a buyer, incumbent, internal builder, or competitor. Investigate it with real people.

AI-generated objections are hypotheses. An assistant’s agreement or score is not buyer validation.
Approval, comprehension,
and impact are different.

A narrative can be approved internally and untested with buyers. It can be understood without changing behavior. Track what each test establishes, and choose the next learning step accordingly.

For commercial measurement, record the baseline, comparison, timing, and other changes. Use comprehension and qualified engagement as leading signals; investigate pipeline, conversion, adoption, and revenue with appropriate attribution.

+ Narrative design in the age of AI

Make AI language earn its place.

A strong AI narrative connects a meaningful change in the audience’s world to a credible way forward.

  • Make AI language earn its place.“AI-native,” “AI-powered,” and “agentic” need a specific explanation of what works differently and why it matters.
  • Find the change beneath the technology.Show how behavior, expectations, or work are changing, and what that means for your audience.
  • Give people a practical role in the future.Explain what AI handles, where human judgment matters, and how responsibilities change.
  • Match proof to the promise.Distinguish what is possible, what the product delivers today, and what customers have actually achieved.
  • Contribute a point of view.Use observations and experience to challenge an assumption and recommend a different decision. Make forecasts recognizable as forecasts.

What will the audience understand or do differently after hearing this—and why should they believe you?

+ Put the story to work

A shared argument.
Useful in the moments that matter.

Build agreement early.Make the strategic choice clear before asking everyone to approve the copy.

For a company narrative, involve leadership in the point of view and the commitments behind it. Product can challenge feasibility. Sales and customer teams can challenge relevance. Agree who owns the work and who makes the final decision.

Where sponsorship is limited, start with an authorized product, campaign, or use case. Use the draft, practical application, and feedback to build support.

Sales and customer conversations

Connect the buyer’s situation to the new approach, then demonstrate the relevant mechanism and proof. Add discovery, technical detail, economics, and next steps around the core narrative. The five-beat prototype is not the entire sales pitch.

Marketing, launches, and thought leadership

Use launches to show how a shipped capability advances the approach. Use thought leadership to teach the idea, explore its implications, and make a useful recommendation. A short asset can express one part of the narrative without reciting the whole arc.

Product and analyst conversations

Use the narrative to examine buying criteria, market direction, product priorities, and where the proposed approach remains difficult. Invite analysts to challenge the premise and interpretation. Preserve the distinction between a future hypothesis and the capabilities customers can use today.

Maintain a durable foundation

Keep the audience, thesis, approach, product scope, terminology, proof, and decision status easy to find. Adapt depth and examples to the audience while keeping the argument consistent.

Refresh examples and evidence when they change. Revisit the core story when the premise, audience, alternatives, differentiated value, or product contribution materially changes.
