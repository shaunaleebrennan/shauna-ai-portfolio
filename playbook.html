My point of view
When competitors tell the same story, they reinforce the same buying criteria. Narrative design should challenge those criteria—with a market insight customers recognize and an approach your business can credibly lead.

01 Find the insight

Start with a decision.
Find a reason to rethink it.

Use narrative design when buyers understand what you sell but miss why it matters, or when your category’s familiar story hides your value.

Before you writeWho are you trying to reach, and what should they understand or do differently?

Positioning explains who should choose your product, over which alternatives, and why. Narrative gives them a reason to see the problem differently. Messaging turns that argument into claims, explanations, and proof.

Choose a specific audience and buying situation. Establish what they do today, where your product helps, and what evidence supports that value.

Customer signal

Look at real decisions, workarounds, objections, and unmet expectations.

Competitive signal

Compare the promises and buying criteria everyone repeats, including reasons to keep the current approach.

Market signal

Investigate changes in work, expectations, and technology use. Check what they mean for this audience.

Separate what you observed from what you think it means. Choose an insight that could change a buying decision, and look for evidence that challenges it.

02 Build the argument

Five beats.
One connected argument.

Marcus Andrews’ five-stage method builds the case for a new approach before introducing the product.

One fictional example runs through the method: a SaaS company’s customers have moved from single-team onboarding to enterprise rollouts. The scenario and product capabilities are illustrative.

The world movesWhat changes for us?
Find a shift the audience can recognize.

The Change

What has changed for your audience?

Identify a change in how customers work, what they expect, or what they need. Be specific about who it affects and what evidence supports it.

Example

“Your customers used to get started within one team. Now an enterprise rollout needs security approval, a data integration, and several teams to be ready together.”

Go deeper: apply and test this step
  • Define the audience, timeframe, and situation. “AI is changing everything” is too broad to do this work.
  • Distinguish a new technical possibility from actual adoption and its consequences.
  • Use customer behavior, product use, credible research, or concrete examples. Investigate contrary cases.

The reasoning. Starts with a specific change in the customer’s work. It gives the story a reason to exist beyond a product launch.

The pressure test. Has onboarding actually become more interdependent for the buyers you want to reach? Compare recent journeys with earlier ones.

The Impact

Why does that change matter?

Explain how the change affects a result your audience cares about. Show what becomes harder or newly possible before introducing the product.

Example

“Each team can finish its checklist while the customer still cannot use what they bought. Until those pieces come together, the value they signed up for stays out of reach.”

Go deeper: apply and test this step
  • Show the mechanism between the change and the consequence. Urgency words cannot supply that connection.
  • Include opportunity alongside the cost of continuing as they are.
  • Give the stakes a human scale: a missed handoff, an unresolved question, or an ambition someone cannot achieve.

The reasoning. Connects added dependencies to a consequence the customer recognizes. It changes what “complete” needs to mean.

The pressure test. Where does a finished internal task still leave the customer blocked? Look for a real handoff and its consequence.

The New Game

What should people do differently?

Teach a practical approach. Explain what people should do, how they will judge success, and what trade-offs it involves. Make it useful without your product.

Example

“Plan onboarding around the customer’s first valuable outcome. Agree what that outcome is, work back to the milestones that enable it, and measure progress against them.”

Go deeper: apply and test this step
  • Define the principle, the actions it changes, and what success looks like.
  • Compare it fairly with the current approach. Acknowledge the work or trade-off it introduces.
  • Make the method useful before a product purchase. “Use our software” leaves the strategic work unfinished.

The reasoning. Offers a practical approach before introducing software. A team can try it with the tools it already has.

The pressure test. Can a team name the outcome, identify its dependencies, and tell whether the customer has reached it?

Why It’s Hard

What gets in the way?

Identify the real barriers to adopting the approach. Include the constraints your product cannot solve.

Example

“That takes a shared view of work spread across customer success, IT, and the customer’s own team. When a requirement is missing, someone needs to see the gap and know who can resolve it.”

Go deeper: apply and test this step
  • Separate familiar frustrations from barriers to adopting the proposed approach.
  • Include costs and risks created by the change itself.
  • Recognize capable people working within real constraints. Some obstacles require process, services, or organizational change.

The reasoning. Explains what makes the new approach difficult. The obstacle follows from the method rather than appearing to justify a feature.

The pressure test. Do customers struggle to see the gap, resolve ownership, or both? Software alone will not settle unclear responsibilities.

The Solution

How does your product help?

Connect the product to the barriers you established. Explain how it changes the work, why buyers should choose it, and what proof supports the result.

Example

“Our onboarding software brings requirements, owners, and milestone progress into one shared view. AI flags missing information so the responsible person can investigate what is holding the customer back.”

Go deeper: apply and test this step
  • Establish why the product is a credible choice against the buyer’s actual alternatives.
  • Distinguish delivered capabilities, configuration, pilots, and plans.
  • Show what the customer can do, what the result depends on, and the next useful action.

The reasoning. Gives the product a defined job: make gaps visible and actionable. The customer’s first valuable outcome remains the goal.

The pressure test. Can the product identify the relevant gap and help the right person act? Demonstrate that workflow before claiming faster onboarding.

04 Apply & test

Will it hold up
with real buyers?

Test the argument before polishing the language. Start with its weakest assumption.

Do they recognize it?

Ask buyers about a recent situation. Does the change or tension appear in their experience without prompting?

Can they explain it?

Ask them to retell the story. What should they do differently, and how does the product help?

Why choose your product?

Compare with their actual alternatives. A useful approach still needs a specific reason to buy.

Can you prove the promise?

Check the relevant workflow and result. A demo shows behavior under those conditions; business impact needs appropriate measurement.

Internal approval, buyer understanding, and commercial impact answer different questions. Record what you have tested and what remains unknown.

+ Narrative design in the age of AI

Make AI language earn its place.

Explain what changes for people, how the product helps, and why they should believe you.

  • Explain what works differently.Give “AI-powered” or “agentic” a specific task, mechanism, and benefit.
  • Find the change beneath the technology.Show how work or expectations are changing for your audience.
  • Give people a practical role.Explain what AI handles, where judgment matters, and who owns exceptions.
  • Match proof to the promise.Separate what is possible, delivered today, measured, and planned.
  • Offer a point of view.Challenge an assumption and recommend a useful decision. Make forecasts recognizable as forecasts.
+ Put the story to work

One argument.
Useful across your GTM.

Agree the strategic choices with the people responsible for them. Then adapt the emphasis to the audience and channel.

Sales

Connect the buyer’s situation to the approach. Demonstrate the relevant product workflow and proof.

Marketing & launches

Use campaigns to teach the idea and launches to show how a shipped capability advances it.

Product & analysts

Test the market interpretation, buying criteria, and product priorities behind the story.

Keep the audience, core argument, product scope, proof, and owner in one shared source. Update the story when those foundations change.
